*Coen Brothers*’ feature debut **Blood Simple** (1984) sucks us into its dark view of the world immediately.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1024/1*t2GhcUiVz1DHgtJz1Eg0uw@2x.jpeg)

1. **Authenticity**

They know their material really well. The characters and the dark view of the world is a consistent and multi-layered start to finish. It’s a product of confidence. They’re confident about their story and the characters.

2. **Concept**

**A detective looks for a man’s wife to see if she’s cheating or not.** This is a story we all are familiar with. But in this movie the point of view is different. And this is the thing, **it’s the same but different.** Every time I look for or study a successful feature debut I see this pattern. The concepts are all very same but they all different at the same time. We’re always familiar with the subject but never make anything of it. I never realized this until I start writing about this movie and its screenplay.

3. **Characters**

The characters and the dialogues between them feel natural. Complex, multi-layered characters are always the ones that the actors are interested in. The movie is full of tense situations and powerful moments such as Marty’s monologue to Ray when he says their situation is ‘funny’.

They’re not cartoon characters, circumstances keep pushing them. Marty attempts to drag Abby outside but she breaks his finger and kicks him in the groin causing him to vomit. Then, Marty goes to meet Visser to tell him, he wants them dead.

Their conscience is never clean, Marty wants them dead but not at the same time. Ray wants her but doesn’t trust her. Abby also wants Ray but doesn’t trust him.

On the page six, there is a line that describes Loren Visser:

> He is LOREN VISSER, a large unshaven man in a misshapen yellow  
> leisure suit.

This one line actually says a lot about him. Using of ‘s’s and ‘l’s over the sentence and using ‘o’ and ‘e’ in both ‘Loren’ and ‘yellow’. And don’t miss the using of the ‘leisure’. **It describes Loren Visser a lot like a snake than a man.**

Visser came to meet him about the pictures of his wife caught cheating, a very cliché scene. Even Marty hits a cliché line like “which rock to turn over.” But at that moment script stops and Visser starts laughing. Remember, using the same but using it differently. Than, Visser turns to leave saying one of the best lines ever:

*Visser — Well, gimme a call whenever you wanna cut off my head… I can crawl around without it…*

BOOM! Coens show how it’s done, baby! You catch that snake pay off? I rarely see as subtle setup and payoff as this one. It’s so good!

4.**Tension, suspense**

Coens always keep us one step ahead of the characters in this journey as we go along. We know more than the characters we see on screen. But at the same time, things always outguesses us. They know exactly when to give and when to hold the information to us to keep us on the edge of our seats.

The scene where Visser kills Marty; Marty wants to take the photograph but Visser wants it back. And we know, at least guess the fact that Visser didn’t kill Ray and Abby and it’s a fake picture. Then, suddenly Visser kills Marty.

If you look back at the scene and deconstruct it, you start to see how they build the tension and suspense over the scene.

Visser didn’t kill them, Marty is about to take the picture, Visser is about to kill Marty, the sweat on his forehead, the lighter which he forgets there etc. Everything about it is so well structured.

5. **Twists and turns**

The incinerator. When a scene introduces the incinerator to us, we start expecting some dead body to burn in there. Even Marty mentions it to Visser. But it never happens, instead, Marty ends up dead and Ray drags him to bury him alive. We think that Marty or Visser is going to kill them but instead Visser kills his client, Marty. Things go so wrong that you won’t even believe but you still witness it. It’s that dark…

Even when Abby pins Visser’s hand down with a knife. We feel he can’t get out of there. It seems impossible but he starts banging on the wall. He gets himself out of there too.

6. **Structure**

The incidents that follow one another doesn’t feel that much of a structured piece. It leans heavily on the themes; the view of the dark world, the misunderstandings. And it connects with the screenwriting perspective that’s saying everything must go wrong.

**Bonus**.

7. **The fate**

Fate is always a character in Coen Brothers’ movies. In this movie, the unfortunate events which the fate causes connect with the view of the dark world and the misunderstandings. **And when everything starts connecting one another you start to feel like you’re watching a moral tale. You start wondering what the story is saying. It leaves an impact on us, on the audience.**
