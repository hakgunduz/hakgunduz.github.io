It’s always fascinating to me that, in comedy series, they hide the plot of the story really well behind all the jokes. You don’t notice the unraveling story behind the things happening all around you while watching.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1280/1*3bHcAWAlJRhPmTCKy1X0Cw@2x.jpeg)

In this story at Brooklyn’s 99th Precinct, murder happens and it is an engine that moves the story forward but it’s in the background like a white noise machine.

So, first of all, that’s racist. Second if all, the story comes from Jake and Captain’s rivalry. It all starts with Jake making fun of the Captain doing the robot voice and accusing him to be a rule following robot. And Captain asks him to wear a necktie which Jake doesn’t take seriously.

Jake WANTS things to be as it is, but Captain WANTS change. He wants this precinct to be the best in Brooklyn. But in order for their wants, they need to learn some things.

Jake NEEDS a mentor who will guide him and redirect his energy and passion for the childish things.

Captain NEEDS to acknowledge the team as they are before making a judgment. You can’t make people do things without knowing anything about them.

Captain starts with Jake at the end of act one. He sends him to the ”unknown”. Door duty and Records Room are not the places Jake familiar with. First, Jake does his own thing and leaves the door duty to find the murderer, and he actually is — but the murderer gets away.

Captain sends him to the Records Room where Jake finds who the murderer is. He starts to learn.

At the last scene, where they are on a takeout, Jake learns the actual reason behind Captain’s seriousness. The captain is gay and has an enormous pressure from everywhere, people wait for him to fail. Jake feels bad. And learns that the Captain is not a rule following robot, he has reasons.

They catch the bad guy, and Jake once again learns why Captain asked for him to wear a necktie. Because they’re a team, and they caught the bad guy as a team. With this, Captain acknowledges him. Jake is not a child. He has reasons to be like that too.

The story ends with mutual acknowledgment.

One other thing that I like about the script is how they used the flashbacks as punchlines. They do this all the time, it’s like their signature.

Jake mentions the old Captain McGintly and flashback — Jake and Rosa, in roller chairs. Holding fire extinguishers. Everyone cheers.

McGintly approaches.

McGintly

What the hell are you doing? (Is he really do whatever they want?)

Jake

Fire extinguisher roller chair derby. (Oh, he actually doesn’t try to lie or anything.)

McGintly

Okay. (the punchline — he literally let’s then do whatever they want.)

Rosa

…and go!

They spray the extinguishers and roll across the floor.

And I liked what they did with the character Charles, he’s kind of weird and gross guy, you can easily not like him. But — when you see Charles, you almost always feel bad for him, whatever he does. This way you can’t stop sympathize with him.

It’s a great comedy series, I recommend watching it, and reading the script — **Brooklyn Nine-Nine** (Pilot) by **Dan Goor & Michael Schur** — for educational purposes only.
