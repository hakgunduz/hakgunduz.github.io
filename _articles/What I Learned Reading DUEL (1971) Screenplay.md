**Duel** (1971) is written by **Richard Matheson**, based off of his short story. It’s **Steven Spielberg**’s feature debut. And it’s one hell of a ride. Literally.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*XwftCvMwUgcoK9J1P6X8UA@2x.jpeg)

Couple of things you notice first when you open the screenplay; act breaks, camera directions and chunks of description.

Maybe act breaks are there because it’s a TV movie. It says so too on the title page.

There are camera directions, because again it’s a TV movie and it’s an old movie. There are camera directions in screenplays before but not much lately. People seem to avoid them and recommend not using them. It makes sense because you’re not writing for the director you’re writing for it to be read. So, make your scripts readable and avoid camera directions at all costs! — just joking. Use them if you have a reason.

Chunks of description and so little dialogue because we only watch MANN (our protagonist) and the TRUCK (antagonist) most of the time.

**At first, I couldn’t see how the acts are functioning but after I read twenty or so, I noticed. It’s differentiating with Mann’s decisions.**

**ACT 1**

There’re detailed descriptions of Mann and the truck in the first scene. I didn’t see that much description in a screenplay ever. But it doesn’t bore. It draws an image in readers head. And you’ll need that image in order to engage people.

Intro of the protagonist, Mann. He’s a husband and a father. And he has to make it to an appointment. We all experienced this before. We all have appointment to attend and we know that feeling. And Mann is being an everyday person makes it more engaging. We’re invested in him right off the bat.

Intro of the antagonist, the Truck Driver. He’s willing to do anything as possible to stop him going to that appointment.

At first, Mann passes the truck then the truck passes his car. It’s irritating but not extraordinary. Near the end of the act, Truck Driver waves at Mann from his window saying he can pass. Mann starts to accelerate. But sees the Blue Sedan coming from the opposite lane. Driver wanted him to crash the Sedan head on… At the end of act one, we understand that this Driver is willing to do anything. He can even kill Mann. We start to expect everything from him after this point.

Simplest plot ever. First act establishes everything we need to know to start this journey.

**ACT TWO**

Second act begins with Mann’s first attempt — **his first decision**. He passes the truck even though he needs to go off the road. Then comes the **consequence**, the truck catches up with him and passes Mann’s car. Mann loses his car’s control and skids to a stop at a cafe.

He gets out of his car and goes to the cafe.

**ACT THREE**

Mann accepts his fate — **another decision**. He’s never going to make it to that appointment. Heads to the Men’s Room and washes his face. Then, comes back and order something to eat. Everyone at the cafe is watching him. But, he notices the truck and the trailer out the window… **consequence** — Truck Driver is there.

The screenplay doesn’t show Truck Driver ever. It gives this scene a huge sense of suspense. Truck Driver is there among the men. Which one?

When you create some kind of suspense as this one with an everyday character, it’s more engaging. Because we feel as if we’re there. Same goes with horror. It’s more terrifying because protagonists are always everyday people, not special ones.

Mann wants to pick up a fight. He wants to tell the driver to knock it off. He goes and talks to some people but they’re not the actual driver. Then, he hears the roar of the engine and notices the truck. Driver is in the truck waiting for him all the time. Truck drives off. Mann runs after it, swallowing the dust and cursing at him.

**ACT FOUR**

Back at the original desire. Mann calls Forbes saying that he’ll be late. Change of plan, thinking it’s over with the truck now.

But no — truck is waiting for him.

**Decision** — Mann doesn’t pass the truck instead follows him until he finds a spot. Then, he goes for a telephone booth to call the police. **Consequence** — Truck makes a U-turn and heads straight for the telephone booth. Crashing it to the ground and shattering the snake cages. Mann notices the truck and bails out at the last second.

**ACT FIVE**

Mann gives up again. Sleeps in his car. But after couple of hours, he notices the truck is waiting for him. He wants to pass the truck but Truck Driver cuts his way.

**decision** — Mann gets out. Stops a passerby car and tells an old couple to call the police. **consequence** — truck heads for Mann’s car. And stops right before crashing.

Mann has enough.

**ACT SIX**

Mann’s next **decision** starts the actual race. He passes the truck and plans to never slow down. And it works at first. But the truck keeps up with him.

A billboard: SLOW DOWN! WE DON’T LIKE TO GET OUR COSTUMERS THAT WAY! **JENSEN’S MORTUARY.**

They hit the gravel road, pass the ROAD REPAIRS. Steams start coming from his car’s hood. His car starts to fail Mann. Even it’s engine stops at some point. Truck closes the distance in the meantime.

Mann drives toward the edge and bails out the the last second. Truck collides with his car and the car and the truck goes down the slope.

EXPLOSION.

Last image — Mann’s face. Not dread, not regret, not sickened withdrawal. Instead, expression of primeval victory!

Mann’s **changed**.
