**Sidney** **Lumet**’s *The Verdict* opens with a scene where our protagonist, Joe Galvin is playing pinball. But he isn’t enjoying it. Instead he gazes at the machine, reaches for his beer and sips it. He’s just filling the spare time between his other sip with the game.

![](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*rpX3V6jII5LDjrWovxXydA.jpeg)

This scene is not in the screenplay. It’s just in the movie. And I think it shows how powerful Lumet’s visual storytelling abilities are. A masterclass in action.

What do we need to learn about this character? He’s unhappy, alcoholic and detached from his environment. Snap. Right off the bat, we’re invested in the character.

Later on, this little detail comes back.

Galvin sees the chance to salvage his career and self-respect by taking a medical malpractice case to trial rather than settling.

While on it, he feels alive. And Sidney Lumet uses this little detail to show us the transformation of the protagonist. Galvin plays pinball again after he decides to take the case to trial, this time he is enjoying it and he barely sips his drink.

This character intro reminded me of **James** **Mangold**’s movie *Cop Land*. There’s a similar scene in there.

> A SKOTCH AND BEER MUG upon A ‘LETHAL WEAPON’ PINBALL MACHINE. Danny Glover and Mel Gibson leap about a blinking triptych of high octane police action. The score tallies into the millions. Strips of tape line the side of the machine, noting high scores, almost all of which are attributed to — Freddy.FREDDY HEFLIN (42) EYELIDS HEAVY, LEANS LOW OVER THE MACHINE. Fingers the buttons, he paddles the silver ball skillfully. A belly hanging over his belt, he is a slump-shouldered man, puffy, wilted. Dull with booze, HIS EYES ARE FOCUSED ON —A NEARBY TABLE —

Again, what do we need to know about this character? He’s an alcoholic, and loser in the real world so he plays pinball all the time. He doesn’t enjoy playing it. His eyes are focused on the — outside. He wants something else. He needs something else… We get this. We get this feeling when we see it.

This kind of little details on character intros gets us feeling for the character. We connect the dots in our minds and find ourselves already related to them. Then, we start rooting for them whole throughout their story.
